The context that shapes recovery

Social workers are often best placed to change what surrounds the person, not only the person.

Recovery capital

Recovery capital is the sum of resources a person can draw on: human (skills, health, hope), social (supportive relationships), physical (housing, income, transport) and community (belonging, access to services, reduced stigma). Raising recovery capital is core social work business.

Housing, income and the justice system

  • Unstable housing makes treatment, medication and appointments harder; secure housing improves outcomes.
  • Poverty and debt create chronic stress and limit options.
  • Criminal justice involvement can create or disrupt treatment. Risk of overdose is high after release from custody.

Families, children and safety

Substance use affects partners, children and extended family. Substance use alone does not mean a child is unsafe; assess actual risk, protective factors and supports. Parents often fear that seeking help will lead to child removal, so be honest about your role and process, and work collaboratively. See the Dani case study.

Stigma and access to care

Stigma, including from health and welfare workers, is a barrier to care. People who feel judged delay help-seeking. Language matters: prefer "person with an alcohol use disorder" to "alcoholic", "substance use" to "abuse", "return to use" to "relapse" in conversation where appropriate. See the language guide.

Culture and community

Experiences of colonisation, racism, migration and discrimination shape both risk and recovery. Culturally safe practice involves working with community, valuing cultural strengths, and using culturally appropriate services, including Aboriginal community-controlled health organisations where relevant.
