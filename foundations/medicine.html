Addiction medicine

What social workers need to know about the body and brain, so you can spot risk, support treatment and refer at the right time.

Educational content onlyThis page does not replace medical advice. If someone may be in withdrawal, intoxicated and unwell, or has overdosed, call emergency services (000 in Australia) or seek urgent medical help.

Substance use disorder as a health condition

Substance use disorder is diagnosed on a spectrum (mild, moderate, severe) using criteria such as loss of control, craving, continued use despite harm, tolerance and withdrawal. It is shaped by genetics, development, trauma, mental health and environment. It is treatable.

Reward, stress and the changing brain

Substances act on the brain's reward and learning circuits, especially dopamine pathways. With repeated use, the brain adapts:

  • Reward: cues linked to use gain power; everyday pleasures feel muted.
  • Stress: when not using, the stress systems become more reactive, driving negative feelings that use relieves.
  • Control: planning and impulse control, which rely on the prefrontal cortex, can be weakened, especially under stress or craving.

These changes help explain why "just stop" is not a plan, and why many people can recover with time and support.

Withdrawal: who needs medical support

SubstanceTypical withdrawalRisk
AlcoholTremor, sweating, anxiety, nausea, insomnia; in severe cases seizures and delirium tremensCan be life-threatening. Seek medical assessment before stopping if drinking heavily or daily.
BenzodiazepinesAnxiety, insomnia, tremor, seizuresCan be life-threatening. Reduce gradually under medical supervision.
OpioidsMuscle aches, nausea, diarrhoea, sweating, agitationRarely fatal but very distressing; medications help. Overdose risk rises after withdrawal because tolerance drops.
StimulantsFatigue, low mood, increased sleep and appetite, cravingsNot usually medically dangerous, but depression and suicidality need attention.
CannabisIrritability, poor sleep, low appetite, restlessnessUncomfortable, generally not dangerous.
Practice ruleIf someone is heavily dependent on alcohol or benzodiazepines and wants to stop, do not advise stopping suddenly. Arrange a medical assessment.

Medicines you will see

Opioid agonist treatment (OAT): methadone, buprenorphine
Prescribed for opioid use disorder. Reduces cravings, withdrawal and illicit opioid use, and lowers mortality. Social workers help with access (dosing logistics, costs, transport) and challenge stigma.
Naltrexone
Blocks opioid receptors and reduces drinking in alcohol use disorder. It must not be taken by someone currently using opioids, as it can precipitate withdrawal; it also blocks opioid pain relief.
Acamprosate
Used after alcohol withdrawal to support abstinence and reduce craving.
Nicotine replacement and other smoking cessation medicines
Often forgotten: tobacco is a major cause of death among people in addiction treatment.
Naloxone
Rapidly reverses opioid overdose. Short-acting, so call emergency services even after giving it. Safe to give to anyone who might be overdosing on opioids.

Overdose basics

Signs of opioid overdose include unresponsiveness, slow or absent breathing, blue lips and pinpoint pupils. Call emergency services, give naloxone if available, support breathing and place the person on their side. Overdose risk is higher after prison, hospital or any break in use, when mixing substances (particularly with alcohol or benzodiazepines) and when using alone.
