Addiction psychology

Why behaviour persists, why change is hard, and how people come to change.

Learning, habit and cue reactivity

Substance use is learned. Using is reinforced by relief or pleasure, so it becomes linked with cues: people, places, moods, times of day. Cues can trigger craving automatically. Over time, behaviour shifts from goal-directed to habitual, which is why people can use even when they do not want to.

  • Positive reinforcement: using feels good.
  • Negative reinforcement: using removes distress or withdrawal.
  • Extinction and new learning: cues lose power when not followed by use, but old learning is not erased, which is why lapses can happen long after stopping.

Ambivalence and motivation

Most people feel two ways about their use. Ambivalence is normal, not denial. The transtheoretical model describes stages people move through: precontemplation, contemplation, preparation, action, maintenance, often cycling back. Arguing for change tends to make people defend the status quo; exploring their own reasons tends to move them.

Co-occurring mental health conditions

Substance use and mental health conditions such as depression, anxiety, PTSD, bipolar disorder and psychosis frequently occur together. Each can worsen the other. Aim for integrated care: do not require someone to be "clean" before treating mental health, or to be "stable" before treating substance use. Always assess suicide risk.

Trauma and adverse experiences

Many people who use drugs have experienced trauma, including childhood abuse and neglect, violence and loss. Substances may serve to cope with intrusive memories or numb distress. A trauma-informed approach prioritises safety, choice and collaboration. See trauma-informed practice.

Shame, identity and recovery

Shame predicts withdrawal from help; self-compassion supports change. Identity matters: people who see themselves as "an addict" can feel stuck, while valued roles (parent, worker, friend) support recovery. Recovery is defined by the person, and can include abstinence, reduced use and improved wellbeing.
